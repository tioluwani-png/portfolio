Most founders want to build before they sell.

That’s the mistake.

We launched **[Relaks](http://relaks.co)** with a ₦2,500 TikTok ad and a coloring book we weren’t even sure people wanted. No strategy deck. No logistics plan. No CRM. We just needed to know one thing — will someone pay for this?

By the next morning, 50,000 views. Ten orders overnight.

That was the plan.

---

We printed the first 100 books within a week. My partner found the printer, sourced the packaging, and handled everything. We made a video. Sold them. Made another video. Sold 200 more.

We weren’t scaling. We were chasing.

And in that chase, Nigeria started teaching us things no business school would.

---

## Lesson 1: The Funnel Breaks the Moment You Say “Click the Link”

The first lesson came fast, right there in the DMs.

We tried the website thing. It works but for analytics, for looking like a real business, for feeling like you have your life together. But if it’s your only channel in Nigeria, you’re bleeding sales every single day, and you don’t even know it.

Here’s the reality: Nigerians are **impulsive** buyers.

Not as an insult. As a fact, you need to build around.

The window between “I want this” and “I’ve moved on” is maybe 30 seconds. You send someone to a website in those 30 seconds — the page loads slowly, there are three steps before checkout, they have to create an account — and the impulse is dead. They close the tab. They forget you existed.

But if they comment “how much?” and you respond in five minutes with a price and a payment link? They buy. Right there. No thinking. Just momentum.

So we stopped redirecting people away from where they found us. Instagram DMs. WhatsApp. Twitter DMs. TikTok DMs. Every platform became a checkout. That decision alone changed our numbers.

Be where your customer already is. Don’t make them travel to you.

---

## Lesson 2: Distribution Is Just Trust at Scale

Then something happened that we didn’t plan for.

Orders started coming from outside Lagos. Abuja. Port Harcourt. Delta. Places we had no way to reach without customers paying painful waybill fees.

We had two options. Try to handle everything ourselves and collapse. Or build a network of people who believed in the product enough to stake their own money on it.

We went with the network.

Distributors bought at ₦6,500. Retail was ₦10,000. Simple math — ₦3,500 margin per book. Attractive enough that people found us. The first one reached out from Asaba. Then Port Harcourt. Abuja. Ibadan. Suddenly, we had ground presence in the South-South, Southeast, North, and Southwest without hiring a single person.

Customers no longer pay heavy delivery fees from Lagos. We just referred them to the distributor closest to them.

And these weren’t delivery agents. They were invested. It was their money on the line, their reputation in their city. They sold harder than any ad we could have run.

Distribution isn’t a logistics problem. It’s a trust problem. Solve the trust, and the logistics follow.

---

## Lesson 3: The Chaos You’re Not Ready For Will Find You Anyway

I want to skip this part. But I won’t.

While all that growth was happening, the inside of the business was held together with tape.

Two people running everything — operations, customer support, fulfillment, communication, building. When a delivery went wrong, we were the ones on the phone. When a customer was upset, we were the ones typing the apology at midnight.

For the first four or five months, we didn’t even have proper financial records. We were selling. Money was coming in. But we were too buried to reconcile properly. We just knew we weren’t broke.

That’s not a business. That’s a controlled emergency.

Then, within that first year, Instagram took our account down.

I won’t get into the full story. But that period broke something in us temporarily. Sales dropped. We were spending money just trying to get back to zero. We almost quit. Not “let’s take a break” **QUIT**. Genuinely almost walked away from the whole thing.

We didn’t. We got the account back. But it taught us something permanent — when everything lives on one platform, you don’t own your business. You’re renting it from an algorithm. And algorithms don’t care about your rent.

Build owned channels. Build redundancy. Before you need it.

---

## The Part Nobody Tells You

Test fast. Spend small. Go to your customer — don’t wait for them to find you. Build systems before the chaos demands them.

That’s what selling physical products across Nigeria beat into us.

But here’s the thing — everything I just described was only the first half of the education.

Because after many months of selling books hand to hand, DM to DM, city to city, our customers started asking for something we hadn’t built yet. And what they asked for turned Relaks from a product into a platform.

That story — how a physical brand becomes an app is the next essay.
